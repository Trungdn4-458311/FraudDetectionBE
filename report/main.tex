\begin{abstract}
Payment fraud causes substantial financial loss and erodes customer
trust on e-commerce platforms. Acting as the risk-analytics function of
a platform, this project answers a concrete business question:
\emph{which transactions are likely fraudulent, and how should the
platform balance blocking fraud against approving legitimate orders
without unnecessary friction?} We combine \textbf{two data sources}: the
simulated mobile-money dataset \textbf{PaySim} ($6{,}362{,}620$
transactions, fraud rate $0.129\%$) and \textbf{nine contextual
features} generated in Python with Faker (account age, new-device flag,
shipping/billing mismatch, IP-to-billing distance, etc.). The pipeline
covers exploratory data analysis, documented before/after cleaning,
feature engineering with explicit class-imbalance handling and feature
selection, and the training of \textbf{three classifiers} (Logistic
Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost) over \textbf{two feature spaces}
(Base/Full), evaluated with metrics suited to imbalanced data (PR-AUC,
Recall, $F_1$) plus a \textbf{cost-based metric} used to select an
operating threshold. A central methodological point drives the design:
the nine synthetic features are generated \emph{conditioned on the
label}, which introduces \textbf{label leakage}; rather than ignoring
this, we quantify it through a systematic Base-vs-Full comparison. Our
strongest models reach PR-AUC $>0.99$ (Random Forest Base: $0.9974$
with a single false positive out of $828{,}659$ legitimate
transactions), and a cost-optimal threshold of $t^{*}=0.21$ stops
$\approx$$100\%$ of fraud value at a false-positive rate of $0.031\%$.
Critically, we show that these near-perfect scores largely reflect the
\emph{easiness} of the data---PaySim encodes fraud with a
near-deterministic balance signature---rather than genuine real-world
generalization. We argue this honest framing, not the headline score,
is the substantive result.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} fraud detection; class imbalance; PaySim;
synthetic data generation; label leakage; PR-AUC; cost-sensitive
learning; operating threshold; XGBoost; Random Forest.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation and business context}
E-commerce platforms process thousands of transactions per minute.
Even when the fraud rate is well below $5\%$, the absolute loss is
large because fraudulent transactions are systematically
higher-value than legitimate ones: in our data the mean fraudulent
transaction is $1{,}467{,}967$ units versus $178{,}197$ for legitimate
ones---roughly $8.2\times$ larger. Fraud therefore imposes a double
cost: direct financial loss, and reputational damage that erodes
customer trust.

Crucially, the countermeasure is not free. Every transaction the
platform blocks is a potential legitimate order rejected---a false
positive that creates \emph{customer friction}, abandoned carts, and
churn. The analytics problem is therefore not ``maximize detection''
but \emph{find the operating point that minimizes total business
cost}. This framing motivates our use of a cost-based metric
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}) rather than accuracy alone.

\subsection{Problem statement and KPIs}
\label{subsec:kpi}
We frame the task as \textbf{binary classification} on a
\textbf{severely imbalanced} dataset: flag high-risk transactions in
real time while limiting the number of legitimate customers blocked.
We track three key performance indicators:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fraud rate} --- the proportion of fraudulent
    transactions. Measures the imbalance severity and the scale of
    exposure.
    \item \textbf{False-positive rate (FPR)} --- the proportion of
    legitimate transactions wrongly blocked. This is the direct proxy
    for customer friction.
    \item \textbf{Financial loss avoided} --- the monetary value of
    fraud stopped by the model. This translates model quality into
    business value.
\end{itemize}

Across the full dataset the fraud rate is $0.129\%$ ($8{,}213$ of
$6{,}362{,}620$ transactions), and the \textbf{total money at risk} is
$\approx 1.21\times 10^{10}$ units. These baseline figures anchor the
cost analysis in Section~\ref{sec:cost}.

\subsection{Contributions}
This report makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{reproducible end-to-end pipeline} from raw
    transactions to a threshold-calibrated classifier, covering data
    generation, cleaning, feature engineering, and evaluation.
    \item A \textbf{leakage-aware experimental design}. Because the
    synthetic features are generated from the label, we build two
    feature spaces (Base/Full) and train every algorithm on both,
    turning a potential methodological flaw into a measurable quantity.
    \item A \textbf{cost-based operating-point selection} that converts
    the Precision/Recall trade-off into money and reports the resulting
    financial KPI.
    \item An \textbf{honest critique of our own results}: we
    demonstrate that near-perfect scores are an artifact of dataset
    easiness and identify precisely which signal drives them.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Report structure}
Section~\ref{sec:formulation} formalizes the problem.
Section~\ref{sec:data} (Module~1) describes both data sources and the
synthetic generation procedure. Section~\ref{sec:eda} (Module~2)

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote the
transaction dataset, where $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the
feature vector of transaction $i$ and $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is the fraud
label ($1 =$ fraud). We seek a scoring function
\begin{equation}
f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{d} \rightarrow [0, 1], \qquad
\hat p_i = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i) \approx P(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i),
\label{eq:scorer}
\end{equation}
and a decision threshold $t \in [0,1]$ producing the hard decision
\begin{equation}
\hat y_i = \mathds{1}\bigl[\hat p_i \ge t\bigr] =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \hat p_i \ge t \quad(\text{flag as fraud}),\\[2pt]
0 & \text{otherwise} \quad(\text{approve}),
\end{cases}
\label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
where $\mathds{1}[\cdot]$ is the \emph{indicator function}, equal to
$1$ when its condition holds and $0$ otherwise. Choosing $t$ is thus a
business decision, not a statistical one---a point developed in
Section~\ref{sec:cost}.

The dataset is severely imbalanced, with positive rate
\begin{equation}
\pi = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i = 0.00129 \;(0.129\%).
\label{eq:imbalance}
\end{equation}
Under such imbalance, accuracy is degenerate: the trivial classifier
$\hat y_i \equiv 0$ achieves $99.871\%$ accuracy while detecting no
fraud. We therefore evaluate with Precision, Recall, $F_1$, and
PR-AUC (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}).

Two aspects distinguish our formulation from generic classification.
First, the \textbf{costs are asymmetric and instance-dependent}: a
missed fraud costs the transaction amount $a_i$, whereas a false alarm
costs a roughly fixed operational amount. Second, part of the feature
vector is \textbf{synthetically generated from $y_i$}, so
$\mathbf{x}_i$ is not conditionally independent of the label in the way
a real deployment would guarantee---the issue we address by
partitioning the features (Section~\ref{subsec:spaces}).

\section{Data and Synthetic Generation (Module 1)}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source 1 --- PaySim}
PaySim is an agent-based simulation of mobile-money transactions
calibrated on real financial logs. The version used here contains
$6{,}362{,}620$ transactions over $743$ hourly steps
($\approx$ 31 days). Table~\ref{tab:schema} lists the schema.

\paragraph{The fraud mechanism in PaySim.} Fraudulent agents in the
simulation take control of a customer account, \emph{transfer out the
full balance}, and then cash it out. This behaviour has an important
consequence exploited later: fraud is confined to \texttt{TRANSFER} and
\texttt{CASH\_OUT} and leaves a characteristic balance signature (the
origin account is emptied), which the balance-error features of
Section~\ref{subsec:balance} capture almost deterministically.

\subsection{Source 2 --- synthetic contextual features}
The project requires supplementing the base data with contextual and
behavioural attributes generated in Python. We produce nine fields with
the Faker library plus custom business logic, using a fixed seed
(\texttt{SEED = 42}) for reproducibility. Generation proceeds at two
levels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Account level} (keyed by \texttt{nameOrig}):
    \texttt{account\_age\_days}, \texttt{home\_country},
    \texttt{device\_fingerprint}. These are drawn once per account and
    merged back onto transactions, so that all transactions of an
    account share a consistent profile.
    \item \textbf{Transaction level}: \texttt{hour\_of\_day},
    \texttt{shipping\_billing\_mismatch}, \texttt{is\_new\_device},
    \texttt{failed\_payment\_attempts}, \texttt{ip\_billing\_mismatch},
    \texttt{ip\_billing\_distance\_km}. These are drawn per row.
\end{itemize}

To make the synthetic signal realistic rather than pure noise, the
risk-related fields are drawn from \emph{label-conditional}
distributions---for example, an unfamiliar device is far more likely on
a fraudulent transaction. Listing~\ref{lst:gen} shows the pattern.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonstyle, caption={Generating
transaction-level features conditioned on the label (excerpt).},
label={lst:gen}]
fraud = df["isFraud"].to_numpy(bool)
# new device: higher probability if fraud
p_dev = np.where(fraud, 0.55, 0.08)
df["is_new_device"] = (rng.random(n) < p_dev).astype("int8")
# failed payment attempts (Poisson)
lam = np.where(fraud, 2.2, 0.12)
df["failed_payment_attempts"] = rng.poisson(lam).astype("int16")
# IP-to-billing distance conditioned on the mismatch flag
dist = np.where(ip_mismatch, rng.uniform(500, 8000, n),
                             rng.uniform(0, 80, n))
df["ip_billing_distance_km"] = np.round(dist, 1)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Label leakage: statement of the problem}
\label{subsec:leakage}
Table~\ref{tab:sanity} is a double-edged result. It confirms the
generator produced a realistic-looking risk signal, but it also makes
explicit that these features were \emph{drawn from the label}. Formally,
for a synthetic feature $s$ we sampled
$s_i \sim \mathcal{P}(\cdot \mid y_i)$, so $s_i$ is informative about
$y_i$ \emph{by construction}, with a separation we chose rather than
discovered.

In a real deployment these attributes (unfamiliar device, address
mismatch, IP distance) \emph{are} legitimately observable at scoring
time and are exactly the signals a fraud system uses---so including
them is not conceptually wrong. The problem is that their measured
\emph{strength} here is an artifact of our generation parameters. Any
performance uplift they produce is therefore an optimistic upper bound,
not evidence of real-world capability.

We address this not by discarding them but by \textbf{partitioning the
feature space} and measuring the difference
(Section~\ref{subsec:spaces}).

\subsection{Balance-error features}
\label{subsec:balance}
For a well-formed transaction, the post-transaction balance should
reconcile with the pre-transaction balance and the amount. We define
two \emph{balance-error} features that measure the violation of this
accounting identity:
\begin{align}
\texttt{errorBalanceOrig} &= \texttt{newbalanceOrig} + \texttt{amount} - \texttt{oldbalanceOrg}, \label{eq:errOrig}\\
\texttt{errorBalanceDest} &= \texttt{oldbalanceDest} + \texttt{amount} - \texttt{newbalanceDest}. \label{eq:errDest}
\end{align}
A value of $0$ indicates a perfectly reconciled transaction; nonzero
values indicate that money appeared or vanished, which is exactly the
footprint of the simulated fraud mechanism.

For contrast, the naive indicator ``origin account emptied''
($\texttt{newbalanceOrig} = 0$) is weak: its fraud rate is $0.223\%$,
only $1.7\times$ the global $0.129\%$. The balance-\emph{error}
formulation is far more discriminative, and indeed
\texttt{errorBalanceOrig} dominates model importance
(Section~\ref{subsec:importance}).

\subsection{The legacy rule-based baseline}
\label{subsec:flagged}
PaySim ships an \texttt{isFlaggedFraud} column representing a legacy
rule-based control. Its behaviour is instructive
(Table~\ref{tab:flagged}): it fires on only $16$ transactions, all of
which are genuinely fraudulent. It therefore achieves \textbf{perfect
precision ($1.000$) but essentially zero recall ($0.0019$)}, for an
$F_1$ of $0.0039$---it misses $99.8\%$ of fraud.

\subsection{Synthetic risk signals}
The fraud rate rises sharply when any risk flag equals $1$, and the
distributions of \texttt{account\_age\_days} and
\texttt{ip\_billing\_distance\_km} separate cleanly by class, exactly
as designed (Table~\ref{tab:sanity}). One synthetic field is
\emph{not} leaky: \texttt{hour\_of\_day} is derived deterministically
from \texttt{step} and therefore reflects a genuine temporal pattern of
the simulation.

\subsection{EDA summary}
The analysis yields five actionable conclusions carried into modeling:
(i)~severe imbalance mandates PR-AUC/Recall over accuracy;
(ii)~fraud is confined to two transaction types, enabling a filter;
(iii)~balance-error features are highly discriminative;
(iv)~\texttt{isFlaggedFraud} is unusable as a feature and weak as a
baseline; and (v)~the synthetic signals separate strongly but carry
leakage that must be handled explicitly.

\section{Data Cleaning (Module 3)}
\label{sec:clean}

We clean under a \emph{before/after documentation} principle, recording
at each step both the number of rows removed and---critically---the
number of \textbf{fraud} rows removed, since the positive class is rare
and must not be silently eroded.

\subsection{Missing values and duplicates}
No column contains missing values, and there are no fully duplicated
records. We check duplicates on the \emph{original PaySim columns}
only: because the synthetic fields are drawn per row, including them
would make every row unique by construction and render the check
vacuous. \textbf{Action: none required.}

\subsection{Invalid values}
We test two business constraints: \texttt{amount} $> 0$ and all
balances $\ge 0$. This identifies $16$ violating rows, which we remove.
Notably \emph{all $16$ are fraud} ($16/8{,}213 \approx 0.19\%$ of
positives)---a reminder that label-blind cleaning rules can still touch
the rare class. We deliberately do not exempt fraud rows from the rule,
as conditioning cleaning on the label would constitute data snooping.
The impact is negligible.

\subsection{Outlier treatment}
Applying the standard $1.5\times$IQR rule to \texttt{amount} yields an
upper fence of $501{,}720$ and flags $338{,}077$ transactions
($5.31\%$) as outliers. \textbf{We keep them all.} The justification is
quantitative: the fraud rate \emph{within} the outlier group is
$1.14\%$, about $8.8\times$ the global rate of $0.129\%$. Clipping the
tail would therefore delete a disproportionate share of the rare
positive class. In this problem a large amount is \emph{signal}, not
noise---a case where the textbook outlier rule is actively harmful.

\subsection{Merchant destination accounts}
Destination accounts prefixed \texttt{M} (merchants) constitute
$33.81\%$ of transactions, and $100\%$ of them have
$\texttt{oldbalanceDest} = \texttt{newbalanceDest} = 0$: PaySim simply
does not track merchant balances. Zero fraud targets a merchant. This
is a \emph{design characteristic}, not corrupt data; we retain these
rows and note that it explains systematic \texttt{errorBalanceDest}
offsets in that subgroup.

\subsection{Velocity and recency features}
The project brief requires risk signals such as transaction velocity
per account and time since last purchase. We construct two causal
features: after sorting by (\texttt{nameOrig}, \texttt{step}),
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{txn\_count\_acct} --- cumulative count of the
    account's transactions up to and including the current one;
    \item \texttt{time\_since\_last\_txn} --- hours since the account's
    previous transaction ($-1$ if none).
\end{itemize}
Both use only past information, so neither leaks future data.

\paragraph{An honest negative result.} On PaySim these features are
nearly degenerate: \texttt{txn\_count\_acct} has mean $1.00$ (75th
percentile $1$, maximum $3$) and \texttt{time\_since\_last\_txn} is
$-1$ for the majority of rows. The reason is structural---origin
accounts almost never recur in the simulation. The features are
implemented correctly and satisfy the requirement, but they carry
almost no information here, which is confirmed independently by both
mutual information (Table~\ref{tab:mi}) and model importance
(Table~\ref{tab:importance}, both $= 0.000$). On a real platform with
returning customers they would be far more valuable.

\subsection{Encoding and scaling}
The only categorical predictor, \texttt{type}, is binary after
filtering and is encoded as \texttt{type\_TRANSFER}
($1 =$ TRANSFER, $0 =$ CASH\_OUT). Identifier columns
(\texttt{nameOrig}, \texttt{nameDest}) are dropped to avoid
memorization, and \texttt{isFlaggedFraud} is dropped for the reason
given in Section~\ref{subsec:flagged}. Because monetary features span
several orders of magnitude, Logistic Regression is fitted inside a
pipeline with \texttt{StandardScaler}; the tree ensembles are
scale-invariant and use raw values.

\subsection{Class-imbalance strategy}
\label{subsec:imbalance}
We address imbalance with \textbf{cost-sensitive class weighting}
rather than resampling, applied consistently across all three
algorithms. For XGBoost the weight follows the standard ratio
\begin{equation}
\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} = \frac{\#\{y = 0\}}{\#\{y = 1\}}
 = \frac{1{,}933{,}537}{5{,}738} \approx 337.
\label{eq:spw}
\end{equation}
Logistic Regression uses \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} and Random
Forest \texttt{class\_weight="balanced\_subsample"}. We prefer
weighting over SMOTE for three reasons: it does not fabricate synthetic
positives in a dataset that already contains synthetic components; it
leaves the test distribution untouched; and it is substantially cheaper
on $1.9$M training rows. Combined with the type filter
(Section~\ref{subsec:filter}), imbalance is addressed at both the data
and the loss level.

\section{Model Development and Evaluation (Module 5)}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Algorithm portfolio and rationale}
We train three algorithms, each on both feature spaces, for six fitted
models in total. All use a fixed seed of $42$. The three are chosen to
span distinct \emph{inductive biases} rather than to stack similar
learners:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Logistic Regression} --- a linear model, serving as
    an interpretable baseline. It answers the question: \emph{how much
    of this problem is linearly separable?}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest} --- a \emph{bagging} ensemble that
    reduces variance by averaging many deep, decorrelated trees.
    \item \textbf{XGBoost} --- a \emph{boosting} ensemble that reduces
    bias by fitting trees sequentially to the residual errors of its
    predecessors.
\end{itemize}

This spread is deliberate: the two tree ensembles can represent
axis-aligned nonlinear interactions (such as the balance-error
footprint of Section~\ref{subsec:balance}), whereas the linear model
cannot. The performance gap between them therefore becomes
\emph{diagnostic evidence} about the geometry of the decision boundary,
a point we exploit in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Logistic Regression}
The model estimates the log-odds of fraud as a linear function of the
features:
\begin{equation}
\hat p_i = \sigma\bigl(\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_i + b\bigr),
\qquad
\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},
\label{eq:logreg}
\end{equation}
fitted by minimizing the class-weighted cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} c_{y_i}
\Bigl[\, y_i \log \hat p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - \hat p_i) \Bigr],
\label{eq:ce}
\end{equation}
where $c_k$ is the class weight defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:imbalance}.

Two implementation details matter. First, the decision boundary is a
\emph{hyperplane}: the model can only express monotone, additive
effects of each feature. Second, because the monetary features span
roughly eight orders of magnitude, the model is fitted inside a
pipeline with \texttt{StandardScaler}; without standardization the
gradient-based solver converges poorly and the regularization penalty
would be applied unevenly across features.

We expect this model to \emph{underperform}, and it is included
precisely for that reason. The fraud footprint is an arithmetic
relation among three columns (Eq.~\ref{eq:errOrig}) rather than a
weighted sum of them, so no hyperplane in the raw feature space can
isolate it cleanly.

\subsection{Random Forest}
A Random Forest averages the predicted probabilities of $B$ decision
trees:
\begin{equation}
\hat p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \hat p_b(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad B = 100,
\label{eq:rf}
\end{equation}
where each tree $\hat p_b$ is grown on an independent bootstrap
subsample and, at every split, considers only a random subset of
features. This double randomization \emph{decorrelates} the trees, so
that averaging cancels their individual variance while preserving their
low bias.

Our configuration reflects the scale of the data
($1.9$M training rows): \texttt{max\_depth=16} and
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=5} bound tree size and hence memory, while
\texttt{max\_samples=0.5} grows each tree on half the rows, roughly
halving training time at negligible accuracy cost. With $d = 11$ Base
features, the default \texttt{max\_features="sqrt"} rule considers $3$
candidate features per split.

The inductive bias suits this problem well. Each split is an
axis-aligned threshold, so a rule of the form
``$\texttt{errorBalanceOrig} \approx 0$ \emph{and}
$\texttt{newbalanceOrig} = 0$'' is expressible in a couple of levels,
and depth $16$ leaves ample room to refine it.

\subsection{XGBoost}
Gradient boosting builds an \emph{additive} model in which each new
tree corrects the errors of the current ensemble:
\begin{equation}
F_M(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \eta\, f_m(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad M = 300,\; \eta = 0.1,
\label{eq:xgb}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the learning rate that shrinks each tree's
contribution. Trees are fitted by minimizing a regularized objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} l\bigl(y_i, \hat y_i\bigr)
 \;+\; \sum_{m=1}^{M} \Omega(f_m),
\qquad
\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \lVert \mathbf{w}\rVert^2,
\label{eq:xgbobj}
\end{equation}
with $T$ the number of leaves and $\mathbf{w}$ the leaf weights, so
that complexity is penalized explicitly.

Whereas Random Forest reduces \emph{variance} by averaging independent
trees, boosting reduces \emph{bias} by fitting trees in sequence. Each
tree is consequently shallow (\texttt{max\_depth=6}) because the
ensemble gains expressiveness through depth of \emph{composition}
rather than depth of individual trees. Stochastic regularization is
applied via \texttt{subsample=0.8} (row sampling) and
\texttt{colsample\_bytree=0.8} (column sampling). We set
\texttt{eval\_metric="aucpr"} so that internal evaluation optimizes
precision--recall area rather than error rate, consistent with our
primary metric (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}).

A practical property motivates its selection for deployment
(Section~\ref{subsec:recommendation}): $300$ depth-$6$ trees form a
compact structure. Our exported deployment artifact occupies
approximately $0.85$\,MB, loads essentially instantly, and scores a
single transaction in well under a millisecond---whereas a forest of
$100$ depth-$16$ trees grown on $1.9$M rows yields a substantially
larger object with a correspondingly slower cold start.

\subsection{How class imbalance enters each objective}
\label{subsec:weighting}
All three models handle the $1{:}337$ imbalance through
\emph{cost-sensitive weighting} of the training loss rather than
resampling, but the mechanism differs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Logistic Regression} (\texttt{class\_weight="balanced"})
    weights each class inversely to its frequency,
    $c_k = N / (K N_k)$ with $K = 2$ classes. On our training split
    this gives $c_1 \approx 169.0$ and $c_0 \approx 0.501$.
    \item \textbf{XGBoost} (\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}) multiplies the
    gradient contribution of every positive example by the ratio in
    Eq.~\ref{eq:spw}, $\approx 337$.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest}
    (\texttt{class\_weight="balanced\_subsample"}) recomputes balanced
    weights \emph{within each bootstrap sample}, so the weighting
    adapts to the positives that happen to be drawn into that tree.
\end{itemize}

These are the same correction expressed differently. Note that
$c_1 / c_0 \approx 169.0 / 0.501 \approx 337$, exactly the XGBoost
ratio: both impose an effective $337{:}1$ penalty for missing a fraud
relative to raising a false alarm. This consistency means the three
models are compared on equal footing with respect to imbalance
handling, and any performance difference is attributable to the
hypothesis space rather than to the loss weighting.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}
With $TP$, $FP$, $FN$, $TN$ denoting the confusion-matrix cells:
\begin{align}
\text{Precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, &
\text{Recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \label{eq:pr}\\
F_1 &= \frac{2\,\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}, &
\text{FPR} &= \frac{FP}{FP + TN}. \label{eq:f1}
\end{align}
Our primary metric is \textbf{PR-AUC}, computed as average precision
\begin{equation}
\text{PR-AUC} = \sum_{k} \bigl(R_k - R_{k-1}\bigr)\, P_k,
\label{eq:prauc}
\end{equation}
where $P_k, R_k$ are precision and recall at the $k$-th threshold.
PR-AUC is preferred to ROC-AUC under extreme imbalance because it
ignores the enormous $TN$ count and therefore does not flatter a model
that succeeds mainly at recognizing the majority class---an effect
visible in our results, where Logistic Regression Base attains ROC-AUC
$0.9782$ but PR-AUC only $0.5932$.

\subsection{Interpreting the ranking: hypothesis space and calibration}
\label{subsec:ranking}
Two aspects of the ordering in Table~\ref{tab:models} deserve
explanation, because both are informative about the problem rather than
about implementation quality.

\paragraph{Why the linear model collapses.} Logistic Regression Base
reaches ROC-AUC $0.9782$ but PR-AUC only $0.5932$. The two numbers are
not contradictory: with $828{,}659$ negatives, a model can rank most
negatives below most positives (high ROC-AUC) while still producing
tens of thousands of false positives among its top-ranked predictions
(low PR-AUC). The root cause is representational. The fraud signature
is the arithmetic relation of Eq.~\ref{eq:errOrig}, an
\emph{interaction} of three balance columns; a hyperplane over those
columns cannot isolate it, so the model compensates by lowering its
threshold across the board. This is exactly the diagnostic value of
including a linear baseline.

\paragraph{Why Random Forest edges out XGBoost.} On the Base space,
Random Forest attains PR-AUC $0.9974$ versus XGBoost's $0.9944$, yet
the gap in \emph{precision at $t = 0.5$} is far larger ($0.9996$ vs
$0.9204$; $1$ vs $211$ false positives). The discrepancy between a
small ranking gap and a large threshold gap identifies the cause as
\textbf{calibration, not ranking ability}. XGBoost multiplies every
positive gradient by $\approx 337$
(Section~\ref{subsec:weighting}), which deliberately inflates predicted
probabilities toward the positive class; consequently many negatives
land above $0.5$ even though they are still ranked below the true
positives. Random Forest's probabilities, obtained by averaging $100$
tree votes, remain closer to the empirical class proportions.

Two conclusions follow. First, comparing models at an arbitrary
threshold of $0.5$ penalizes aggressively reweighted learners for a
property that is trivially fixable. Second, this is a concrete
motivation for the threshold optimization of Section~\ref{sec:cost}:
the right question is not ``which model is best at $0.5$?'' but ``which
model, at \emph{its own} best operating point, minimizes business
cost?''

\section{Cost-Based Threshold Selection}
\label{sec:cost}

\subsection{The cost model}
The default threshold $t = 0.5$ is a statistical convention with no
business meaning. We instead select $t$ by minimizing expected cost,
exploiting the asymmetry established in Section~\ref{sec:formulation}:
a missed fraud loses the full transaction amount, whereas a false alarm
costs a fixed operational amount $C_{FP}$ (manual review plus customer
friction). Formally,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Cost}(t) = \underbrace{\!\!\sum_{i:\, y_i=1,\, \hat p_i < t} \!\! a_i}_{\text{fraud value lost (FN)}}
\;+\; \underbrace{C_{FP}\cdot \bigl|\{i:\, y_i=0,\, \hat p_i \ge t\}\bigr|}_{\text{friction cost (FP)}},
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $a_i$ is the amount of transaction $i$. We set $C_{FP} = 10$
units as a stated modeling assumption and sweep
$t \in \{0.01, 0.02, \ldots, 0.99\}$.

We apply the analysis to \textbf{XGBoost Base}---the honest,
deployment-suited model (Section~\ref{subsec:recommendation}).

\subsection{Results}
The cost curve attains its minimum at $t^{*} = 0.21$, well below the
default (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Moving from $0.5$ to $0.21$ reduces
total cost by $16.6\%$.

recovers considerably more in prevented fraud value. Because the
average fraud is worth $\sim$$1.47$M, catching even one extra fraud
justifies thousands of extra reviews---the optimizer is simply
following that arithmetic.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the cost assumption}
\label{subsec:sensitivity}
The optimal threshold depends on $C_{FP}$, which we assumed rather than
measured. The structure of Eq.~\ref{eq:cost} makes the direction of
that dependence clear: raising $C_{FP}$ penalizes false alarms more and
pushes $t^{*}$ upward (fewer blocks, more fraud tolerated), while
lowering it pushes $t^{*}$ down. Because the mean fraud amount
($\sim$$1.47$M) exceeds $C_{FP}$ by five orders of magnitude, the
optimum is strongly biased toward high recall and is \emph{insensitive}
to moderate changes in $C_{FP}$. A production deployment should
calibrate $C_{FP}$ from real review-handling costs and measured churn
following a wrongful block; we flag this as the single most important
assumption to validate.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the scores are near-perfect}
Random Forest Base attains PR-AUC $0.9974$ with one false positive in
$828{,}659$ legitimate transactions. In real fraud detection such
numbers would be implausible, and intellectual honesty requires
explaining rather than celebrating them. The explanation is the data,
not the model.

PaySim generates fraud through a scripted mechanism: the fraudulent
agent empties the origin account. That action leaves an
\emph{almost deterministic} arithmetic footprint in the balance
columns, which \texttt{errorBalanceOrig} (Eq.~\ref{eq:errOrig})
captures directly---hence its $53.7\%$ importance share. The decision
boundary is therefore close to a rule, and any sufficiently flexible
model finds it. Notably, the linear model does \emph{not}, because the
footprint is a nonlinear interaction of three columns: this is why
Logistic Regression Base reaches only PR-AUC $0.5932$ while both tree
ensembles exceed $0.99$. The gap between them is strong evidence that
performance is driven by one nonlinear, near-deterministic pattern.

\subsection{Assessment of label leakage}
Our Base/Full design lets us bound the leakage effect rather than
speculate. Two findings emerge. First, the leakage is \textbf{real}:
synthetic features hold $\approx 28\%$ of XGBoost-Full importance and
lift Logistic Regression by $+0.38$ PR-AUC. Second, it is
\textbf{not load-bearing for the best models}: tree ensembles gain only
$+0.002$ to $+0.005$, and the strongest honest configuration (Random
Forest Base) uses no synthetic features at all. Consequently our
headline conclusions do not rest on leaked information---but any Full
number should be read as an optimistic upper bound.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Simulated data.} PaySim is agent-based, not real
    transactions. Its fraud is more regular, and its balance
    bookkeeping cleaner, than reality. Performance here should not be
    extrapolated to production.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic features are label-derived.} Their measured
    strength reflects our chosen parameters
    (Table~\ref{tab:datadict}), not empirical evidence.
    \item \textbf{Degenerate velocity features.} Because accounts
    rarely recur, the required behavioural features carry no signal, so
    this dimension of the risk model is untested.
    \item \textbf{Random rather than temporal split.} We split
    randomly with stratification. A production system faces
    \emph{temporal} generalization---training on the past, scoring the
    future---which a time-based split would evaluate more faithfully
    and would likely yield lower scores.
    \item \textbf{Assumed friction cost.} $C_{FP} = 10$ is a modeling
    assumption, not a measurement.
    \item \textbf{Single split, no cross-validation.} Results come
    from one stratified $70/30$ split with a fixed seed; we report no
    variance across resamples.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Model recommendation}
\label{subsec:recommendation}
We separate the \emph{best} model from the \emph{deployed} model, a
standard and defensible distinction:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest Base} is the best honest performer
    (PR-AUC $0.9974$, one false positive, no leakage) and is what we
    report as the headline result.
    \item \textbf{XGBoost Base} is our deployment recommendation. It is
    statistically indistinguishable in practical terms
    ($0.9944$ PR-AUC) but yields a far more compact and faster-loading
    artifact, which matters for a real-time API on constrained
    infrastructure. It is also leakage-free.
\end{itemize}
Both decisively outperform the incumbent rule-based control
(Section~\ref{subsec:flagged}), which catches $16$ frauds versus
$2{,}451$ for Random Forest Base.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We built an end-to-end fraud-detection pipeline on PaySim combined with
a synthetic contextual feed, covering all five modules: business
understanding and KPI definition, exploratory analysis, documented
cleaning, feature engineering with imbalance handling and feature
selection, and the training and evaluation of six models under a
cost-based operating criterion.

Technically, the tree ensembles reach PR-AUC $>0.99$, and the
cost-optimal threshold $t^{*} = 0.21$ stops $99.96\%$ of fraud value at
a false-positive rate of $0.031\%$---a $16.6\%$ cost reduction over the
default threshold and a decisive improvement over the incumbent
rule-based control.

Methodologically---and more importantly---the contribution is the
\textbf{Base-versus-Full design}, which converts a latent label-leakage
flaw into a measured quantity, and the accompanying finding that
near-perfect scores here reflect the \emph{easiness of the data} rather
than genuine generalization. We identified the specific mechanism (a
near-deterministic balance signature captured by
\texttt{errorBalanceOrig}) and showed that our best honest model needs
no synthetic features at all. We regard this diagnosis, rather than the
headline metric, as the substantive result.

\paragraph{Future work.} Three directions follow naturally.
\emph{(i)~Deployment (Module~6):} package the model as a real-time API
with an analyst review-queue interface.
\emph{(ii)~Monitoring (Module~7):} track data drift against the
training distribution and define retraining triggers.
\emph{(iii)~Methodological hardening:} adopt a temporal split, add
cross-validation with variance reporting, calibrate $C_{FP}$ from real
operational costs, and validate the pipeline on a genuine transaction
dataset where the balance signature is not near-deterministic.
